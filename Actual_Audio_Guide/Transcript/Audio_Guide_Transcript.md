# Museum of Theft: Audio Guide Transcript

Team Biscuit · November 2026 · updated from the October 2026 draft

## About this script

- **14 tracks**: an entrance welcome, one introduction per section, one track per case, and an exit. Each QR code opens one track, so visitors move at their own pace.
- **Length:** 15:03 of narration in total (2245 words). Case tracks run from about 55 seconds to 1 minute 40; the others run under a minute.
- **Each case track** opens with a hook, tells what happened and what it turned into, gives both sides, and ends on an open question. It does not walk the listener through the objects; at most one line points at something beside the QR code.
- **Sections 1 and 2** leave the verdict to the visitor. **Section 3** keeps the stance of its wall panels.
- **Voice:** Kokoro `af_heart` at speed 0.9, about 149 words a minute including pauses.
- **No sound effects.** The October draft's sound cues (shop bell, drum, forest and chainsaw) are not recorded. Places where they could be mixed in later are marked *[Sound cue]* below; those lines are not read aloud.
- The text of each track lives in `tracks/*.txt`. To change the audio, edit that file and run `generate_audio.py` again.

## Entrance

### Track 01 · Welcome to the Museum of Theft

Entrance, beside the entry poll · 0:55 · 153 words

Welcome to the Museum of Theft.

Before you look at anything, picture a theft. A phone lifted on a crowded bus. A break-in at an empty house. You know who the thief is. You know who lost something. And you know whose side the law is on.

The dictionary on the wall agrees. It calls theft an unlawful taking of property. Simple.

This museum takes that sentence apart, one word at a time. Section one asks: who is the thief? Section two asks: what can be taken? Section three asks: who names the act, and who owns the story?

You are the jury here. At every case, a strip runs from zero, not theft, to ten, theft. Place a pin where you think the case belongs.

One thing before you start. Answer the short poll at the entrance. You'll meet the same questions on your way out, and your answers may not match.

## Section 1: Who Is the Thief?

### Track 02 · Who Is the Thief?

Section 1 header · 0:30 · 88 words

Most of us can spot a thief. Or we think we can.

This section tests that with three questions. Is there an owner? Is there a loss? Is there a law that names the taking as a crime?

In the first case, every answer is yes, and the thief is obvious. After that, each case changes just one thing: who the taker is, what story gets told about him, or how many hands the goods pass through.

The taking never disappears. So listen for what moves the label.

### Track 03 · A Wallet on a Crowded Street

Case 1 · Ordinary Theft · 0:54 · 141 words

Start with the easy one.

A crowded street. A hand slips into a pocket and leaves with a wallet. By the time the owner notices, the thief is gone.

This is theft at its plainest, and it passes all three tests. There is an owner. There is a loss. And there is a law: Section 303 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, India's criminal code. It defines theft as dishonestly taking someone's movable property out of their possession, without their consent. The pickpocket fits every word.

Now notice what that law leaves out. It covers only movable property. Under this section, land cannot be stolen at all. Hold on to that. It comes back in section three.

And the law says nothing about who the thief is, or where the goods end up. The next three cases push on exactly those gaps.

### Track 04 · Two Shoplifters, One Counter

Case 2 · Kleptomania and Class · 1:08 · 169 words

*[Sound cue, not recorded: a shop bell and the murmur of a busy store, fading under the voice.]*

Two women take the same thing from the same shop counter. One goes home. The other goes to court.

It is the 1880s, in London and New York. New department stores lay their goods on open counters, where anyone can touch them. Shoplifting rises. And many of those caught are middle-class women, the stores' best customers, taking things they could easily afford.

Doctors have a word ready: kleptomania, an urge to steal. For a respectable woman, it turns a crime into an illness. And stores, keen to keep good customers, often settle quietly.

New York, 1882. Ellen Sardy is caught in a Sixth Avenue store with three pairs of hosiery and five pieces of silk. Her lawyer admits the theft, and says she was of weak mind. The court accepts it. She goes free.

The historian Elaine Abelson found that this excuse reached only women of means. Stealing by anyone else, she writes, was simple theft.

Same counter. Same goods. Same law. So what decided who was the thief?

### Track 05 · Robbing the Rich

Case 3 · Robin Hood · 1:05 · 181 words

*[Sound cue, not recorded: a single drum under the opening verse. The verse is the opening of A Gest of Robyn Hode, read here in modern spelling.]*

"Lithe and listen, gentlemen, that be of freeborn blood. I shall you tell of a good yeoman: his name was Robin Hood."

That is how one of the oldest Robin Hood ballads begins, more than five hundred years ago. And the Robin in it may surprise you.

He is first mentioned in the 1370s. In the earliest surviving ballads, from about 1450, he is an outlaw who stops travellers on the road and takes what they carry at the point of a weapon. The stories are violent. One ballad says he did poor men much good. But no early ballad shows him handing them his loot.

That famous line, robbing the rich to give to the poor, is first set down plainly in 1592, two centuries after he first appears. Later writers made him a nobleman. Hollywood made him a hero.

Check him against the three tests. Owner, loss, law: all present. By the rules of his own world, Robin Hood was a thief.

The robbery is in every version of the story. What keeps growing is the reason for it.

### Track 06 · The Benin Bronzes

Case 4 · The Benin Bronzes · 1:17 · 190 words

Here is a taking with no obvious thief. Or with too many.

February 1897. A British force captures Benin City, in what is now southern Nigeria. Weeks earlier, a British party travelling to the city had been ambushed, and most of its members killed. Now soldiers remove thousands of objects from the royal palace, among them brass plaques that recorded the kingdom's court. Many are auctioned in London to pay for the expedition.

Then the objects travel. Officers. Auction houses. Dealers. Collectors. Museums. Each later sale was lawful where it happened. So who is the thief? Everyone in the chain can point to someone earlier.

Many people still call it theft. The objects were taken by force, and Nigeria has long asked for them back. In 2022, the Smithsonian transferred ownership of 29 of them. A group in the United States sued to stop it, arguing the bronzes were tied to the slave trade. It lost. In 2023, Nigeria's president declared the Oba, Benin's king, their owner. Who should hold them is still disputed.

This section asked who the thief is. Benin leaves a harder question. Who is the owner?

## Section 2: What Can Be Taken?

### Track 07 · What Can Be Taken?

Section 2 header · 0:38 · 97 words

In the first section, something always went missing. Here, it is hard to say what was lost. That is the puzzle.

Seeds are carried out of a forest. Blood is given in a village. Photos are copied from the internet. And every owner keeps what they started with. Brazil keeps its trees. The donors gave willingly. The photos stay online.

What changes is what each thing becomes: plantations on another continent, data for studies the donors say they never agreed to, a search engine for faces.

You will hear both sides each time. The verdict is yours.

### Track 08 · Seventy Thousand Seeds

Story 1 · Rubber, beside the seed · 1:23 · 192 words

Look at the seed beside you. In 1876, about seventy thousand like it left Brazil. Within forty years, the Amazon had lost its lead in rubber. Was that a theft?

Brazil was then the world's main source of rubber, and Britain wanted a supply of its own. Henry Wickham was an Englishman living in the Amazon. The India Office paid him ten pounds for every thousand seeds. By his own later account, he told customs they were "exceedingly delicate botanical specimens", bound for Kew Gardens in London.

Only a few thousand sprouted. The seedlings went to Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, and their descendants to Malaya. For twenty years, little changed. Then came plantation labour, investment, and a world that wanted tyres. By 1913, Asia's plantations had overtaken the Amazon, and Brazil's rubber boom ended. Kerala's rubber trees descend largely from the same seeds.

In Brazil, Wickham is remembered as the father of bio-piracy. Wickham called it patriotism. No Brazilian law in 1876 banned taking the seeds. The plantations took decades of work to succeed. And in 1920, Britain knighted him.

Brazil kept its rubber trees. So what, if anything, did Britain take?

### Track 09 · What the Blood Was For

Story 2 · Havasupai · 1:34 · 217 words

Imagine giving blood to help doctors fight a disease in your community. Years later, you learn it was also used to study where your people came from.

The Havasupai are a Native American tribe of about 650 people, living deep in the Grand Canyon. Diabetes was common among them. So from 1990, researchers from Arizona State University collected blood from more than two hundred members. Tribal leaders were told the study was about diabetes. The written consent form said something broader: behavioral or medical disorders.

The blood became genetic data. It was used in 23 papers and dissertations. Fifteen had nothing to do with diabetes. They covered schizophrenia, inbreeding, and the tribe's origins. One suggested its ancestors came from Asia. The tribe's own history says they began in the canyon.

In 2003, a member of the tribe, Carletta Tilousi, heard about these studies at a student's thesis defence. People got degrees and grants, she said, and they never asked our permission.

The lead researcher defended the work as ethical. In 2010, the university's governing board settled. It paid $700,000 to 41 tribe members, returned the samples, and apologised. No court ever ruled on whether the research broke the law.

The blood went home. The published papers did not. So how much permission travelled with that blood?

### Track 10 · A Search Engine for Faces

Story 3 · Clearview AI, beside the laptop · 1:37 · 235 words

Think of every photo of you that is online right now. Include the ones other people posted. Now imagine someone takes one new photo of you, and a single search finds all the others.

That search exists. The laptop beside you shows how it works. Clearview AI, founded in the United States in 2017, copies publicly posted photos from social media, news sites and other websites. The people in them are not told, and not asked. Software turns each face into a faceprint: a list of numbers describing its geometry. Upload one photo of a stranger, and the system returns other photos of that face, with links to where they appeared. By the company's own count, it holds more than a hundred billion images. It sells searches mainly to police and government agencies.

Canada's privacy commissioner called this mass surveillance, and illegal. Regulators in Australia and Europe reached similar findings. Illinois law treats a photo and a faceprint differently, and protects only the faceprint. A European privacy group says the data of billions of people was stolen.

Clearview's co-founder answers that all the data inside Clearview AI is publicly available, as it is with Google. The company says police use it to identify suspects and abused children. It admits no wrongdoing.

Nobody lost a photograph. So what, if anything, was taken when the people in them became searchable?

These facts were checked in October 2026.

## Section 3: Who Names the Act and Who Owns the Story?

### Track 11 · Who Names the Act?

Section 3 header · 0:32 · 80 words

In this section, the taking happens on paper.

When land changes hands, the fight often starts with words. Shared land becomes surplus. A forest becomes a set of coal blocks. Clearing it becomes development. And remember section one: under India's criminal code, land cannot be stolen at all.

So here, two records hang side by side: the official one, and the one kept by the people who lost the land. As you read, ask yourself who is holding the pen.

### Track 12 · The Dawes Act

Case 1 · The Dawes Act · 1:17 · 174 words

How do you take ninety million acres? In this case, with a survey, a ledger, and one word.

In 1887, the United States passed the Dawes Act. It let the government divide Native American reservations, held in common by each nation, into individual plots. A family head usually received 160 acres. Whatever was left over got a new name: surplus. And surplus land could be sold to settlers. By 1911, the government was printing posters: "Indian Land for Sale".

Officials called this reform. Senator Henry Dawes and his supporters said private farms would make Native Americans independent citizens.

Native nations saw treaty land disappearing through paperwork. The Cherokee Nation and the traditionalist Keetoowah Cherokees sent protests to Congress, asking that their land stay with the nation as a whole. Redbird Smith led thousands of Cherokees in refusing allotment, and in 1906 he told a Senate committee why.

By 1934, when allotment ended, Native Americans had lost about two-thirds of their land. In the official record, it reads as administration. What would you call it?

### Track 13 · The Hasdeo Arand Forest

Case 2 · Hasdeo Arand, beside the two jars · 1:24 · 191 words

*[Sound cue, not recorded: about ten seconds of forest canopy, then a chainsaw fading in. Use only recordings made with consent and credited in the catalogue.]*

Two jars sit in front of you. One holds forest soil and clear water. The other holds coal dust and murky water. Between them is a forest in central India, and an argument that is not over.

Hasdeo Arand, in northern Chhattisgarh, is one of central India's largest dense forests. It is an elephant corridor, the source of a river, and home to thousands of Adivasi people. Beneath it lies coal.

The official records contradict each other. In 2010, a government study marked the forest a no-go area for mining. In 2012, the Environment Ministry approved a mine anyway, run by Adani Enterprises for Rajasthan's state power company. In 2014, a green tribunal cancelled that approval. The mining went on.

Village councils passed resolutions refusing to give up the forest. One village, Ghatbarra, was granted forest rights in 2013, then lost them in 2016. In 2022, Chhattisgarh's own Assembly voted unanimously to cancel every coal block in Hasdeo. In December 2023, an order went out to fell more than fifteen thousand more trees.

The company calls it responsible development. Every step carried an official stamp. So whose record becomes the history?

## Exit

### Track 14 · Has Your Definition Changed?

Exit, beside the exit poll · 0:50 · 137 words

You came in with a simple definition: an owner, a loss, and a law that names the act. Each section pulled one of those apart.

In the first, the act stayed the same while the label moved. In the second, the owners kept what they had, and the argument was over what it became. In the third, the law did the naming, and the people who lost land kept records of their own.

None of these cases is closed. Benin objects are still being returned. Hasdeo's forest is still being cleared, and defended. Clearview's database is still growing.

So before you leave, answer the poll once more, and compare your two sets of answers. That difference is this museum's final exhibit.

Your pins are part of the record now. Thank you for visiting the Museum of Theft.
